The premise

Sound is an output. Test it like one.

A notification chime, a voice prompt, a beep that confirms a scan, music that should pause when a call comes in. Tests assert on what is on screen and let the speaker go unheard.

Hear it

A timeline of what the device played: sound and silence to a tenth of a second, each sound's pitch and level.

Where the sound comes from

Nothing installed. Nothing on the speakers.

The emulator's own audio stream

read over its control port, which it opens for this machine only, with a token. No sound device on the computer, no app on the device.

Silent on the machine

booted headless with its audio off, the emulator still hands its sound to the capture.

Read in tenths of a second

loudness, and the pitch when one frequency holds most of the sound. A click, speech, noise: sound with no one pitch.

The emulator streams its audio in packets of 20 to 30 milliseconds. Mobium reads it with Go's standard library, places it by the clock so a pause in the stream is silence where it fell, and writes a WAV and a timeline.

Part 2

Assert it

The sounds to hear, in order, each a pitch with an optional length. Anything else fails, saying what was heard.

Part 2: turning the timeline into a test.

At the stop

Pitch and length, in order.

$ mobium audio stop -o seq.wav --expect 440:1.8-2.3,880:1.8-2.3
saved seq.wav: … 440 Hz … 880 Hz …

$ mobium audio stop -o tone.wav --expect 880
error: expected 880 Hz; heard 440 Hz for 2.1s (0.3–2.4s)
— the capture is saved at tone.wav

A wrong expectation fails, says what was heard, and keeps the capture as the evidence. --expect silence asserts nothing was heard; a sound of a fifth of a second or less, like a tap's click, does not count.

The assertion is part of the stop, so it is the same from the command line, in a test file, and from every client. The capture is saved even when the expectation fails: that is when you want to listen to it.

In a test file

One step. Five clients.

{"audio": {"action": "stop", "path": "mobium-report/sequence.wav", "expect": [
  {"hz": 440, "min_ms": 1800, "max_ms": 2300},
  {"hz": 880, "min_ms": 1800, "max_ms": 2300}
]}}
$ mobium test tests/heard.test.json   2 passed (17.3s)
$ mobium test tests/wrong.test.json   0 passed, 2 failed (14.5s)

The same from Python, Java, JavaScript, Go and .NET: a wrong expectation raises NotConfirmed, saying what was heard.

heard.test.json asserts what was played; wrong.test.json expects what was not, and must fail, each test saying what it heard. Both are in the repository and run in under twenty seconds each.

So a test asserts pitch and length, never level — and every answer says the volume it was taken at: “at media volume 0 of 15, where nothing played as media is heard”.

Act 4 of the demo sets the emulator's media volume and puts it back. A silent capture that says volume 0 is a test environment to fix, not an app to blame.

Part 3

Interrupted

A call, an alarm, a notification: what cut across the app, read from Android's own audio service.

Part 3: the moment an app's audio is most likely to go wrong.

Pixel 7 emulator · Android 15

A call came in. Listen.

The tone plays; the emulator rings for five seconds; the tone comes back. The sound is what Mobium captured, laid under the screen recording.

$ mobium call ring
$ mobium call hang
$ mobium audio stop -o call.wav
interrupted: muted for a call 3.7–8.5s,
  a ringtone played 3.9–8.6s
Press play, sound on. The tone, then the ring, and no tone while it rings: Android silenced the app for the call and gave it back after. The app's own line said playing throughout. The screen recording and the capture were started together and aligned by the ring's first moment, 0.7 seconds apart.

Any interruption

Any sound that cuts across the app.

The app muted

for a call, or by the device's or its own volume — with Android's reason.

Another app's sound over it

a ringtone, an alarm, a notification, a voice call, the assistant, navigation, another app's media — each named by what it is for.

A Clock timer's alarm over the tone

emulator: an alarm played 4.4–6.5 s  ·  Pixel 8 Pro: an alarm played 5.3–10.5 s

the alarm, heard
The alarm does not silence the Audio Demo, which asks for no audio focus: its tone plays on under the alarm's louder melody. An app that holds audio focus would pause itself; either way the interruption is reported. A touch's click is not an interruption.

Part 4

On a real phone

Nothing outside a phone hears what it plays. What interrupted the app, it can still tell.

Part 4: the limits, and what is left when the sound is out of reach.

Pixel 8 Pro · Android 17

A phone's sound stays on the phone.

$ mobium audio start
recording what interrupts the app's audio — a phone's sound is not captured
$ mobium audio stop
interrupted: an alarm played 5.3–10.5 s

Any Android phone adb reaches

a device farm's included, with nothing installed on it.

A ring needs a call from outside

which only a farm with a line can place. An emulator rings on demand.

Measured on a Pixel 8 Pro with the owner's permission. Capturing from the shell heard one app's own samples, but a call's mute is the player's own volume, which no such capture sees, and the system's ringtone is kept out of capture altogether. The audio service's log has both, so that is the answer on a phone.

What it cannot do

Said, not approximated.

iOS

refused, saying why: a simulator's sound plays through the Mac, and an iPhone's stays on it.

A phone's sound

not captured; its interruptions are.

Level

follows the device's volume, so it is reported, not asserted.

The system's own sounds

are in the capture: with touch sounds on, a tap is a tenth of a second of sound.

Each limit is a refusal with a reason, or a number in the answer, never a silent pass.
